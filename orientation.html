The Other Half of the Sea

A short orientation, for readers meeting Carthage for the first time

You already know Rome. You have never not known Rome.

Check your phone. July is named for Julius Caesar; August for his heir. September, October, November, December are just Roman counting — seventh, eighth, ninth, tenth. The letters you’re reading came to you from Rome’s alphabet. The words senate, republic, citizen, empire, civic, military, legal — Rome, Rome, Rome. If you speak Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese or Romanian, you speak the evolved dialect of a Roman army camp. Rome isn’t a subject you missed at school. It’s the furniture of your life, so familiar you’ve stopped seeing it — the way you don’t see your own glasses.

Here is the thing the furniture doesn’t tell you: it was very nearly otherwise.

Twenty-three centuries ago, the Mediterranean had two superpowers, not one. On the Italian side: Rome — a land power, a farmer-soldier republic, methodical, stubborn, expanding down the peninsula one road at a time. And across the water, on the coast of what is now Tunisia: Carthage.

Carthage was older than Rome — founded, tradition says, by Phoenician settlers from Tyre a full sixty years before Romulus got around to his wall. It was richer than Rome, probably richer than anywhere. It was a merchant republic whose trading network ran from the Atlantic coast of Africa to the ports of the Levant; its sailors were the best in the world and had been for centuries; its explorers had pushed through the Strait of Gibraltar and down the West African coast while Rome was still a regional concern with no navy at all. Aristotle — no cheerleader for foreigners — analysed the Carthaginian constitution alongside Sparta’s and pronounced many of its arrangements excellent. This was not a pirate cove or a trading post. It was one of the great achievements of the ancient world: a civilisation of harbours, ledgers, libraries, and law.

You know almost nothing about it. That’s not your fault. Almost nothing is what there is to know — and why there is almost nothing is the single most important thing to take with you into the book.

Almost everything you can read about Carthage was written by its enemies.

Not most things. Effectively everything. The Greeks fought Carthage for two centuries over Sicily and wrote about it accordingly. Then the Romans fought Carthage in three wars, destroyed it utterly, and afterwards wrote the histories. There is no Carthaginian account of Carthage. No histories, no poems, no letters, no philosophy, no plays. Not because they weren’t written — a civilisation of that wealth and literacy wrote enormously — but because after the end, nobody copied them. Ancient books survived by being recopied, generation after generation; the books of a people who no longer existed were recopied by no one. What survives is this:

What remains of Carthage, more or less in full:

— Thousands of inscriptions: tombstones and temple dedications. Names, gods, formulas. No books.

— One explorer’s log — Hanno’s voyage down the African coast — surviving in a Greek translation of unknown reliability.

— The texts of a few treaties with Rome, quoted by a Greek historian writing for Roman readers.

— One farming manual: twenty-eight books on agriculture by a Carthaginian named Mago, which the Roman Senate ordered translated into Latin while the city that produced it was being demolished. It survives only in fragments, quoted by Roman writers on agriculture.

— And everything the winners said about them.

That last category is where your impressions come from, whether you know it or not. The Romans remembered Carthaginians as greedy, cruel and treacherous — punica fides, “Punic faith,” was Latin’s standard idiom for treachery, the loser preserved in the winner’s language as an insult — and when Rome’s national epic needed a tragic queen to curse Rome from the walls of Carthage, it borrowed the city’s own founder for the part. Even her story is Roman.

Even the darkest accusation — that Carthaginians sacrificed infant children to their gods — comes to us the same way. Hostile authors assert it; archaeology has produced urns and grave-markers that scholars have now spent a century arguing over; the honest answer is that we cannot fully adjudicate the charge. Not because the evidence is ambiguous, but because of the structural problem underneath every fact here: it is a trial at which the defence never got to speak. That is what total destruction does. It doesn’t just kill a people. It leaves their enemies as the sole custodians of their memory.

So: how did it come to that?

Three wars, over more than a century. The first — which began over Sicily, and whose opening act was so improbable that no novelist would dare invent it — is the business of the book that follows, and will not be spoiled here. What you need is the second, because the second is where you learn that Rome’s victory was not fate.

In 218 BC a Carthaginian general named Hannibal — you’ve heard of him; he’s one of exactly two Carthaginians anyone has heard of, and the other is his father (Dido, as you’ve just seen, doesn’t count; even she is Roman property) — marched an army out of Spain, over the Alps, elephants and all, and into Italy. He stayed there for fifteen years, undefeated. At Cannae, in 216, he annihilated the largest army Rome had ever fielded: somewhere between fifty and seventy thousand Roman dead in a single summer afternoon — a one-day toll European warfare would not see again for two thousand years. Any normal ancient state would have negotiated. Rome refused to even discuss terms while Hannibal stood on Italian soil — possibly the most Roman fact in recorded history — and ground its way back over a decade, and won anyway. But hold on to Cannae. The contest between these two civilisations was real. The other outcome was on the table, repeatedly. The world you live in was one afternoon from being a different world, several times over.

The third war wasn’t a contest at all. Carthage had recovered commercially — being Carthage — and Rome, whose leading statesman ended every speech, on any subject, with “Carthage must be destroyed,” eventually manufactured its pretext. Three years of siege; then, in 146 BC, the city fell, burned for seventeen days, and was systematically demolished. The survivors — tens of thousands, from a city of hundreds of thousands — were sold into slavery. The site was formally cursed. (The famous detail that the Romans ploughed salt into the earth so nothing would grow is a modern invention — it appears in no ancient source, and was inserted into history books barely a century ago. Even our myths about the erasure turn out to be fabricated.)

One detail from the sack survives, and it is worth your attention. The Roman commander, Scipio Aemilianus, watching Carthage burn, wept — and quoted Homer’s lines on the fall of Troy. His companion, the historian Polybius, asked him why. Because, Scipio said, one day the same would happen to Rome. The man conducting the erasure understood, in the act itself, that nothing about the outcome had been inevitable — that winners are merely losers whose turn hasn’t come. It is the closest thing to an honest sentence the whole catastrophe produced.

After 146 there was one superpower, and the sea became what Romans called it with a shrug: mare nostrum. Our sea. Everything you were taught to call “classical civilisation” — the law, the literature, the roads, the languages — is the winner’s half of a pair.

And if you’re tempted to shrug at that — different winner, different month-names, so what? — start with this: the letters were never Rome’s to begin with. Rome wrote its victory in an alphabet descended, through the Greeks and Etruscans, from Phoenician script — the writing system of Carthage’s own mother-culture. The names of things are the wallpaper. What was at stake was the building. Remember the furniture? That was the polite version; furniture can be replaced. Europe is built on the foundations of a soldier-farmer republic — its law, its empires, its idea of what a great life was: load-bearing walls, all of them. Carthage was a different foundation: a merchant republic, whose power ran through routes and contracts rather than land and legions. Not gentler — merchant powers use force plentifully when the margin justifies it — but different in kind. A Mediterranean built on that foundation would not be this history with the rooms repainted. It would be a different building — different rooms, different doors, no promise of anything you’d recognise. How different? That question has no bottom, and this is not the place to jump in. The book will take you down as far as you care to go.

Because here is what Carthage is now: a suburb. A pleasant, leafy, seaside suburb of Tunis — villas and gardens over the rubble field, a UNESCO plaque, ruins you can walk in an hour. The light-rail line from downtown runs out along the coast, and commuters yawn through stations called Carthage Salammbô, Carthage Dermech — and Carthage Hannibal. The man who nearly ended Rome is a stop where people check their phones on the way to work.

But look closer at the platform, because the story has one more turn. The erasure, it turns out, was never as total as Rome intended. The city died and the books died — but the people didn’t. The countryside stayed Punic-speaking for centuries: five hundred years after the sack, Saint Augustine — himself a North African — recorded that when you asked the peasants near Hippo who they were, they answered, in Punic, Chanani. Canaanites. The name their ancestors had carried from Tyre, still in their mouths half a millennium after the city fell. And the modern nation standing on that ground knows it. Tunisia has taken Carthage up deliberately, with pride — Hannibal on the banknotes, the presidential palace at Carthage itself, the airport, the festivals, the founding queen reclaimed from Rome’s epic and restored to her own city. The name on that light-rail sign was put there by the heirs, on purpose.

A name, it turns out, can be reclaimed. A literature cannot. The people are still there; the voice is not. Every word Carthage ever got to say about itself still fits in a box five lines long — and the last line of the box isn’t even theirs. It belongs to the people who killed them.

The book you are about to read is what it might sound like if that silence could answer.
